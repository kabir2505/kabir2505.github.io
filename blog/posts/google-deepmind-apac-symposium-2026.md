I spent two days at Google Ananta for the Student Symposium of the Google DeepMind APAC Research Symposium. I went in expecting talks on the latest models. I came out thinking mostly about how to learn, and how much of that I am quietly handing over to tools.

This post is mostly a record for myself, so I don't forget what I heard. Writing it down also helps me figure out what I actually believe.

#### what the panel kept coming back to

The panel had [Prateek Jain](https://www.prateekjain.org/), [Ranjita Bhagwan](https://research.google/people/ranjita-bhagwan/), [Prof. Animesh Mukherjee](https://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~animeshm/), [Prof. Mausam](https://www.cse.iitd.ac.in/~mausam/) and [Partha Talukdar](https://parthatalukdar.github.io/). The questions were about research careers and AI, but the answers kept circling the same few ideas.

##### Don't let the robot lift for you

The analogy that stuck with me: if a robot bench-presses the weight for you, the bar still goes up, but your muscles don't grow. Using AI while you are learning something for the first time works the same way. The output looks fine, and you have learned almost nothing.

The advice was not "never use AI". It was to have a strong enough value system, and enough self-control, to keep it away when you are building the muscle. Once the tools have done some work, have the discipline to think it through and check whether it is actually correct.

##### Short-term vs. long-term optimization

A lot of the discussion was really about this trade-off. Tools make you faster today. Thinking hard makes you better in three years. The panel was pretty clear that the value of deep thinking is not going away, and that as long as you keep doing good work, opportunities stay open.

##### What will still matter in 3–5 years

Someone asked which skills will persist. Roughly in order of how much the panel stressed them:

1. **Judgement.** The AI will suggest something. You need to know whether it fits your problem.
2. **Picking and framing problems.** What is a good problem? What vantage point should I look at it from? How do I find a good solution, and then argue to others that it is good?
3. **Learning to learn.** [Prateek](https://www.prateekjain.org/) made this point before the debate heated up: picking up new things quickly, and still going deep, will stay valuable.
4. **Adapting to change.** [Partha](https://parthatalukdar.github.io/) talked about being comfortable with uncertainty and making progress anyway.
5. **People.** Talking, collaborating, building a network.

And least important? Writing code from scratch. As someone who has spent a lot of evenings reimplementing papers line by line, that one stung a little. I think the point is that the _understanding_ you get from doing it matters more than the act itself.

##### Rigor, and no shortcuts

Research needs rigor. Expect to fail ten times before something works. There are no shortcuts. And on the PhD question, the answer I liked most was simply that a PhD is a lived experience; you can't fully reason your way into knowing if it's right for you.

[Prof. Mausam](https://www.cse.iitd.ac.in/~mausam/) made the whole thing spicy. He occasionally took deliberately extreme counterpoints against the other panelists, and half the room was laughing most of the time. It also forced everyone to defend their positions properly, which made the panel much better than the usual polite agreement.

#### Talks I enjoyed

**[Manish Gupta](https://www.linkedin.com/in/manish-gupta-4556591/)'s opening keynote.** He had a live conversation with Gemini, switching between Hindi, English and Haryanvi mid-sentence. It was genuinely funny, and honestly a little surprising how smoothly Gemini kept up.

**[Mausam](https://www.cse.iitd.ac.in/~mausam/)'s keynote on AI for materials and medicine.** Close to an hour long, and I didn't look at my phone once. Same energy as on the panel, just pointed at science this time.

**[Aditya Kusupati](https://adityakusupati.com/) on [Matryoshka embeddings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13147).** My favourite session. He walked us through the work patiently, took a lot of questions, and was very casual about the whole thing, which made it easy to engage. The main thing I took away: sometimes a very simple idea is the right answer to a hard problem. You don't always need the complicated version. Given that I'm about to spend six months working on a similar domain, the timing could not have been better.

**[Dheeraj Nagaraj](https://dheerajnagaraj.com/) on generative models (Day 2).** A good look at how this work happens inside DeepMind.

#### Conversations and people

I got some time with **[Partha Talukdar](https://parthatalukdar.github.io/)** and asked him about using AI to find research directions and ideate on niche problems. His answer came back to what the panel kept saying: you build intuition and taste by doing research, over and over. There isn't a tool that gives you that (yet).

I also had long conversations with [Vignesh](https://www.linkedin.com/in/vignesh--n/), a BTech student at IIT Madras working on AI for wireless, about pursuing research, how we each think about it, and where we want our careers to go. Some of the best parts of the two days happened outside the sessions.

The talent density at the event was a bit absurd. Every conversation over lunch or tea ended with me learning something.

#### Closing

I start at Microsoft Research tomorrow. If I had to compress the two days into one note to myself: think before you prompt, verify after, and don't expect shortcuts. Being around that many people doing serious work made me want to work harder, and I'm going to try to hold on to that feeling.
